In this series exploring ancient ethics for modern leadership, we’ve looked at the 'Diamond Pillar' of non-harm and the 'Gold Pillar' of generosity. Now, we arrive at the third, and perhaps most surprisingly relevant, ethical principle: the **Third Precept**.

Traditionally, this is the abstention from sexual misconduct. In the [10 Pillars book](https://www.sangharakshita.org/individual-works/the-ten-pillars-of-buddhism) by Urgyen Sangharakshita, the founder of the Buddhist tradition in which I practice, it is known as the 'Pillar of Crystal' symbolising the purity, transparency, and brilliant clarity that comes from a well-tended inner life.

I know what you might be thinking. What does a precept about sexual ethics have to do with leading an open source project? The answer, I’ve found, has very little to do with sex itself, and everything to do with how we manage our creative energy, our professional relationships, and our own inner state of contentment.

### Creative and relational energy

To understand this precept in our context, we must look beyond its most literal interpretation. This precept, at its root, is grounded in one of the most powerful 'kleshas' or 'poisons' that keep us locked in our own suffering - that of greed. The other two - hatred / aversion and delusion / ignorance - can also play a part sometimes, as well. As a friend of mine, Sanghamani, once shared, this precept is fundamentally about what we do with our desire and creative energy in the broadest sense. For me, it's about ensuring we don't misuse our relational power to cause harm or manipulate outcomes.

In the world of open source where influence is currency, this subtle misconduct can take many forms:

-   Using charisma or flattery to push a technical agenda or get something prioritised.
-   Cultivating unhealthy dependencies, where a contributor’s access or influence is tied to their personal relationship with a leader.
-   Projecting our own needs for validation onto the community, driving them towards goals that serve our ego rather than the project’s health.

The goal is clarity of intention. Our professional interactions should be clean, transparent, and free from the subtle poisons of manipulation and emotional entanglement. This is where the practice of the precept begins.

### Navigating relational dynamics as a woman in tech

As a woman leading in a very male-heavy industry, navigating these dynamics carries a unique weight. The line between being a kind, collaborative leader and having that friendliness misinterpreted is one I, and many other women, walk every day.

There can be an unspoken pressure to use 'soft skills' to soothe egos or to be the 'mother figure' in a community, work that is both exhausting and invisible. Conversely, direct and clear communication can be misread as being 'harsh' or 'aggressive' in a way that it rarely is for male counterparts.

Applying the pillar of crystal for me, therefore, is a practice of radical clarity. It means being impeccable with my relational boundaries. It means being warm, friendly and kind, without leaving room for misinterpretation. And it means cultivating an inner confidence that is not dependent on the approval of others, allowing me to lead with integrity, even when it’s uncomfortable.

### The positive practice of cultivating sustainable creativity and joy

The true power of this precept lies in its positive form: the active cultivation of **Stillness, Simplicity, and Contentment**. Practising these qualities provides a proactive strategy for unlocking a more joyful, creative, and sustainable way of working. The aim is to create the inner conditions for our best work to emerge, not from frantic pressure, but from a place of centered clarity.

**Stillness** is the practice of stepping away from the relentless stream of notifications to create the space where true insight can arise. It is the source of our best strategic thinking, allowing us to see the bigger picture beyond the immediate noise. By consciously making space for quiet contemplation, we move from a state of constant reaction to one of purposeful action. Stillness provides the foundation for deep, focused work.

**Simplicity** is the discipline that leads to elegance and power. In a world chasing the 'next shiny thing,' a commitment to simplicity allows us to focus on the core mission of the project. It is the art of solving problems in the most direct and sustainable way, saying 'no' to feature bloat, and finding the profound strength that comes from doing a few things exceptionally well.

**Contentment** is the deepest source of sustainable motivation. It is the revolutionary practice of finding genuine satisfaction in the work itself and its steady progress. This allows us to derive joy from building a stable, healthy project and community, detaching our sense of success from the volatile metrics of external validation like GitHub stars or download counts. As a teacher in our tradition, Sanghamani notes that an ideal practitioner is 'obviously happy,' finding fulfillment from within. This inner contentment becomes the engine of long-term creativity and joyful persistence in our work.

### Leading with clarity

The pillar of crystal is ultimately about clarity. When our motivations in our professional relationships are clear — free from the murkiness of manipulation and projection — we become trustworthy. When our leadership is grounded in a deep well of contentment, we become stable and resilient.

This inner state of stillness, simplicity, and contentment creates the external conditions for a community that is calm, focused, and healthy. It's a community whose leaders teach, by example, a more sustainable way to be. This, I believe, is one of the most profound gifts a leader can offer.

_How do you cultivate stillness and contentment in your own work? Where do you see the pressure of discontentment showing up most in your community?_
