A few days before this year's Mautic World Conference, a memory popped up on my Facebook. It was a photo from our very first community sprint in Amsterdam, seven years ago almost to the week, when the community was just starting to take ownership of Mautic.

![A group of around 35 people from the Mautic and Drupal communities standing and sitting together behind long wooden tables in a large open room, several of them wearing yellow t-shirts, smiling at the camera.](/assets/img/blog/mwc-2026/mautic-sprint-2019-group.jpg)
*Us with the Drupal folks in Amsterdam in 2019 - we co-hosted our first community sprint at their contribution day*

I put it on the first slide of my keynote, and on 4 November I stood in the Amphitheatre at Sea Containers on London's South Bank and asked who in the room had been there. Five or six hands went up! We've come a really long way in seven years, so I thought I'd share a few of my highlights from the conference and the sprint.

## How far we've come

Back in 2019 I remember us sitting round a table working on Mautic 3, three releases behind on Symfony (the framework Mautic is built on), with people saying we would never be able to catch up.

![A dozen or so people gathered around a round table covered in laptops, coffee cups and yellow sticky notes, some sitting and some standing, in a room with red and grey patterned walls.](/assets/img/blog/mwc-2026/mautic-sprint-2019-at-work.jpg)
*The sprint team at work, Amsterdam, 2019*

That sprint is also where we sketched out our first teams and working groups on sticky notes, and scribbled down on a whiteboard how they would be run and elected.

![A flipchart covered in yellow sticky notes grouped into rows. Each row starts with a team (Product, Marketing, Community, Education, Legal and Finance) followed by its working groups, such as Release, Security, Newsletter, Blog, Website, Events, Forums and Documentation.](/assets/img/blog/mwc-2026/mautic-sprint-2019-teams-flipchart.jpg)
*Teams and working groups, first draft!*

![A whiteboard with handwritten notes in red pen. Under 'By team' it lists one leader, one assistant, and members or contributors attending one in three meetings. Under 'Elections' it lists a first term of six months, then twelve months, one team election every two months, and that people can be elected several times.](/assets/img/blog/mwc-2026/mautic-sprint-2019-governance-whiteboard.jpg)
*How teams are run and elected, as we first imagined it*

This year we shipped one major release (Mautic 7) and two minor releases (7.1 and 7.2), pretty much one a quarter, and Mautic now runs on fully supported versions of everything underneath it. It has also been our biggest year for contributions in the seven years we have figures for. In the twelve months to the end of October there were 11,794 contributions to Mautic, which is 16 times the figure for 2020. About a third of those came from automated tooling (mostly a bot which keeps our documentation up to date), but 7,890 of them came from people, which is almost three times what people contributed the year before!

![Stacked bar chart of contributions to Mautic for each year from 2020 to 2026. The total grows from 720 in 2020 to 2,856 in 2025, then jumps to 11,794 in 2026, made up of 7,890 contributions from people and 3,904 from automated tooling.](/assets/img/blog/mwc-2026/mwc26-contributions-by-year.png)
*Contributions to Mautic year by year, from my keynote slides - the blue part is humans!*

Those 7,890 contributions came from 221 people (a few less than the 252 we had last year), and 63% of that work landed in the core of Mautic with another 21% in our documentation.

![Horizontal bar chart showing where the contributions people made in 2026 landed. 63% in the core mautic/mautic repository, 21% in documentation, 8% in other repositories, 5% off GitHub and 3% in the marketplace.](/assets/img/blog/mwc-2026/mwc26-where-the-work-landed.png)
*Where the work of people landed this year*

As for what that work was, two thirds of what people did this year was reviewing someone else's changes, with most of the rest being pull requests (proposed changes to the code or the docs).

![Horizontal bar chart showing what kind of contributions people made in 2026. 65% were pull request reviews, 29% were pull requests, 3% were Jira issues completed, 1% was forum support and feedback, and under 1% was blog posts, knowledge base and events.](/assets/img/blog/mwc-2026/mwc26-what-people-did.png)
*What people did this year - two thirds of it was reviewing each other's work*

It hasn't been an easy year. We lost around 60% of our funding as sponsors had to cut their own budgets, we ran an emergency fundraiser, and we made some painful cutbacks. My own paid hours went down to three days a week at the start of the year, and I'm now down to half a day a week and volunteering the rest. We are on a path back to stability, but we're not there yet.

Even so, it felt great to be back with the community. Sometimes we get so bogged down in the minutiae that we forget to step back and look at how much things have come on, and I was so proud standing on that stage showing what this community has built.

## Can you Mautic a country?

One of my highlights was listening to Joey Keller (CTO at Friendly) talking about how Mautic powered the communication and community organising for a movement right across Hungary. This was a project he took on himself rather than through the agency, and he'd told me bits about it (and the frustrations!) while it was running.

In March 2024 a new political movement appeared in Hungary almost overnight. Tens of thousands of people turned up to a demonstration, thousands of them offered to help, and the 'volunteer management system' was a single email address which received 15,000 emails in two days. Joey's was one of them. There was no budget, no servers, not even a credit card, and three days until the next rally.

What they could do was spin up Mautic. A form asking people what they could offer took 100,000 registrations on its first day. Over the following months Mautic was used to recruit and onboard 2,830 election observers, to take activists through agreements and GDPR training using campaigns, and to run local groups by treating each one as a company with its members as contacts. That last one was a three week prototype which was meant to last three months and ended up running for ten, with 1,000 groups and 40,000 members. The movement became the Tisza party, which went on to [win the parliamentary election in April 2026 with a two-thirds majority](https://www.osw.waw.pl/en/publikacje/analyses/2026-04-13/elections-hungary-triumph-peter-magyar).

Joey was very clear that Mautic didn't create the movement or the votes, and he was candid that the party no longer uses it. I asked him that in the Q&A! His point was that when hundreds of thousands of people suddenly wanted to take part, Mautic could turn the chaos into structure. He also made the point that you can't build a movement on Facebook, because followers are not an organisation and you don't own the relationship.

Sitting there listening, I was thinking about the power that open source brings. It enables innovation and customisation at next to no cost, you can spin it up immediately, and nobody is counting your contacts and sending you a bigger invoice. Joey's story is the dramatic version, but there are so many others. People have built whole careers around Mautic. Organisations who could never afford the commercial tools are doing better marketing because of it. Others simply want to do their marketing in a way that respects people's privacy. All of that exists because a community of people keep showing up to build it.

## Talking to your Mautic

I was also able to sit in on the talk from Frederik Wouters from Dropsolid, on managing Mautic without logging in, which was built around the idea that people will always find the most convenient option (he got there by way of sliced bread and toilets, which you'll have to watch the recording to understand!).

He has an assistant called Bruce which he can dictate to. He told us about wandering round a museum in Athens speaking his notes into his phone and having a blog post drafted and ready to review by the time he walked out. Then he did it live. One spoken request, and a couple of minutes later there was a segment, a form, an email and a campaign sat in his Mautic instance, plus an article on his website with the form embedded for us to rate his session. It was really cool to watch, and a great demonstration of what Mautic and AI can do together. He has shared his experiments on GitHub, including a plugin which lets AI tools such as Claude or Mistral work with your Mautic directly.

## A sprint full of people giving their time away

The thing I noticed most across both days was generosity. Every speaker had given their time and their knowledge to share what they've learned with everyone else, and on the 5th November, the community sprint was full of people getting stuck in together, with a great atmosphere in the room.

I spent most of my day on our enterprise gap analysis and on some of the privacy project work. The gap analysis is a list of 98 things that large organisations have asked for which Mautic doesn't do yet (or doesn't do well enough), grouped into eleven themes. Two agencies, Friendly and Dropsolid, had already given us their feedback from RFPs (requests for proposals, the long requirement checklists that big organisations send out when they're choosing software) that they'd worked on. At the sprint we had folks from Friendly, Dropsolid and Leuchtfeuer round the same table, telling us how important each feature is to their clients and what priority they'd put on it. That is so much more useful than me guessing from a spreadsheet!

While that was going on the developers were busy too. [33 pull requests were merged into Mautic](https://github.com/mautic/mautic/pulls?q=is%3Apr+merged%3A2026-11-05..2026-11-05) on the day of the sprint and another 36 were opened, covering everything from new quick filters and a big update to the user interface foundations through to bug fixes and documentation.

We always have a snacks table at the sprint, and this year we made it a chocolate table in memory of Rob Kaye, the founder of MusicBrainz, who [died in February](https://blog.metabrainz.org/2026/03/05/remembering-mayhem/). Rob was well known for running the chocolate table at the Google Summer of Code mentor summits. I met him at FOSDEM and through Google Summer of Code, and he was really supportive when Mautic was a new organisation in the programme and I was a new org admin and mentor finding my feet. It was a small thing, but there were some lovely conversations round that table and it felt right to remember someone who gave so much to open source.
